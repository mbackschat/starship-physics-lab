# What will Flight 14 deliver to orbit?

**Pre-registered 16 August 2026. Flight 14 had not launched.**

This is a prediction, not a finding. It is committed before the measurement exists so that the comparison afterwards means something. Everything below is reproducible from the model as it stands today; `git log` proves when.

**Question.** Flight 14 is Starship's first attempt at an operational orbit, carrying Starlink V3 satellites. It will therefore produce the first direct measurement of what Starship actually delivers, which is the number the whole payload argument turns on. What does the model say it will be?

## The prediction

| Dry mass estimate | Assumed ship dry mass | Predicted payload | Starlink V3 units | Total reaching orbit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Wikipedia, V2 | 85 t | 198.6 t | 116 | 298 t |
| Wikipedia, V1 | 100 t | 180.7 t | 106 | 298 t |
| SpaceX target, 2019 | 120 t | 156.8 t | 92 | 298 t |
| What the 100 t claim needs | 160 t | 109.2 t | 64 | 297 t |
| Musk, 2019, measured | 200 t | 61.5 t | 36 | 296 t |
| The article's estimate | 220 t | 37.7 t | 22 | 296 t |

**The strong prediction is the last column, not the third.** Across a 135 t range of assumptions about how heavy the ship is, the total mass arriving in orbit moves by 2.5 t, about 0.8 %. The rocket equation fixes what arrives. Only its composition is in doubt.

So the single falsifiable claim is:

> **Flight 14 will place roughly 296 to 298 t into low Earth orbit**, counting the ship, its residual propellant and its cargo together. Payload is whatever part of that is not ship.

## What would refute what

The asymmetry here matters and it cuts against drawing a conclusion too early.

**A low observed payload proves nothing.** No operator flies a maximum payload on a first orbital attempt. SpaceX will carry a conservative satellite count with margin, so a small number is fully consistent with every row in the table. It is not evidence for the article.

**A high observed payload is decisive**, because the model puts a hard ceiling on it at each assumed dry mass:

| Observed payload | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| More than 198.6 t | **The model is wrong.** No plausible dry mass allows it. |
| 109.2 t or more | Ship dry mass is at most 160 t. The article's 220 t estimate is refuted. |
| 61.5 t or more | Ship dry mass is at most 200 t. The article's estimate is under real pressure. |
| About 37.7 t, *and stated to be a full load* | Consistent with the article's 220 t estimate. |
| Below 37.7 t, or not a full load | Uninformative. Do not read anything into it. |

**The model is also refuted** if the total reaching orbit lands materially outside 296 to 298 t while the vehicle matches the modelled configuration. That would mean the mission budget, the stage masses or the engine figures are wrong, independent of the dry-mass argument.

## Assumptions

These are the ways this prediction could be wrong without anybody having lied.

1. **A 9,404 m/s mission budget**, calibrated in [physics-reference.md](../../docs/physics-reference.md) section 3.3 and cross-checked against Falcon 9 and the Space Shuttle. Flight 14's actual orbit may be lower or differently inclined, which changes the budget and therefore the payload. **Check the achieved orbit before comparing.**
2. **Starlink V3 units mass 1.705 t**, derived from Flight 13's 20 satellites at 34.1 t. Flight 12's 22 units at 37.5 t reproduces from the same figure, which is the only corroboration available. No published spec.
3. **Flight 14 flies the modelled `starship_v3` configuration.** It uses B21 and S41, the third Block 3 pair. If they differ materially from B19/B20 and S39/S40 in dry mass or propellant load, this prediction does not apply to them.
4. **No performance reserve is modelled.** Real flights hold margin back; the model solves for the payload that exactly meets the budget. This biases the prediction high.
5. **The ship is recovered.** Its recovery propellant is counted in the mass reaching orbit, which is why that figure is ~296 t rather than payload plus dry mass alone.

Assumption 1 is the one most likely to invalidate the comparison, and it is the first thing to check when the flight data arrives.
